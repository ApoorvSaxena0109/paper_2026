\begin{abstract}
This paper examines how geopolitical oil shocks are transmitted across sectors and whether aggregate market indices accurately reflect underlying economic disruptions. Using a multi-event framework spanning five major oil-related shocks, we document substantial cross-sectional heterogeneity in sectoral returns that is systematically related to oil exposure. Sectors with higher oil betas experience significantly larger abnormal returns, and this relationship is robust across alternative specifications, placebo tests, and event windows. Dynamic analysis further shows that the effect builds over time, consistent with persistent sectoral reallocation rather than short-term market overreaction.

To quantify the extent of information loss due to aggregation, we construct a bounded Aggregation Masking Index ($\Psi$). We find that masking is significantly stronger during geopolitical crises than during non-event periods, indicating that index-level responses understate economically meaningful heterogeneity. Extending the analysis internationally, we show that cross-country variation in market responses is systematically related to energy import dependence. Overall, our findings highlight the importance of cross-sectional analysis in understanding shock transmission and suggest that aggregate indices may provide incomplete signals for investors and policymakers during periods of geopolitical stress.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Financial economists and practitioners routinely rely on aggregate market indices---such as the S\&P~500---as summary statistics of economic conditions and the impact of macroeconomic shocks. This practice implicitly assumes that index-level movements accurately reflect underlying economic effects. However, when shocks generate heterogeneous responses across sectors, aggregate indices may conceal economically meaningful variation. In such settings, index-level movements can provide an incomplete---and potentially misleading---signal of the true economic impact.

Geopolitical shocks provide a particularly relevant setting in which this issue arises. Such shocks are typically transmitted through specific economic channels---most notably energy prices, supply chains, and trade exposure---affecting industries asymmetrically \citep{kilian2009not,caldara2022measuring,smales2021geopolitical,antonakakis2017oil}. As a result, some sectors benefit while others experience substantial losses. When these heterogeneous responses are aggregated through capitalization-weighted indices, positive and negative effects may offset each other, leading to a muted aggregate response. Consequently, aggregate indices may exhibit a form of aggregation bias, obscuring economically meaningful sectoral disruptions.

We study this phenomenon using a multi-event framework centered on five major geopolitical oil shocks: the 2026 Strait of Hormuz crisis, the 2022 Russia--Ukraine conflict, the 2022 OPEC+ production cut, the 2023 Middle East escalation, and the 2020 COVID-19 demand collapse. These events span both supply-side and demand-side oil shocks, enabling us to test whether the transmission channel operates differently across shock types---a distinction emphasized by \citet{kilian2009not} and \citet{kilian2009impact}.

We exploit the February 28, 2026 Strait of Hormuz disruption as our primary quasi-natural experiment. The Strait handles approximately 21\% of global petroleum consumption daily \citep{eia2023hormuz}, and the crisis was sudden, largely unanticipated, and primarily transmitted through oil price channels, making it ideal for cross-sectional identification. Using cross-sectional regressions on 34 sector and sub-sector ETFs, we show that pre-event oil price sensitivity ($\beta_{\text{oil}}$) strongly predicts cumulative abnormal returns (CARs), with $\gamma_1 = +20.18$ ($p < 0.001$) that survives six increasingly saturated specifications including market risk, macroeconomic factors, and firm fundamentals. This finding generalizes across all five events in our panel.

To quantify the extent to which aggregate indices mask these heterogeneous responses, we introduce a novel bounded measure---the Aggregation Masking Index ($\Psi$). Unlike simple ratios of index-level to sectoral returns, $\Psi$ is bounded on $[0,1]$ by Jensen's inequality and captures the degree to which capitalization-weighted aggregation conceals underlying sectoral variation. Empirically, we find $\Psi \approx 0.947$ across all five events, implying that approximately 95\% of the weighted sectoral return dispersion is netted out through capitalization-weighted aggregation. Because diversified portfolios are designed to cancel idiosyncratic variation, a high $\Psi$ partly reflects the normal functioning of index construction. However, the near-invariance of $\Psi$ across events of very different magnitudes and types suggests that geopolitical shocks systematically generate the kind of mixed-sign sectoral responses that diversification eliminates---an important consideration for those who interpret index-level movements as measures of economic impact.

The paper contributes to several strands of literature. At the broadest level, it documents that aggregate market indices systematically underestimate the economic impact of geopolitical shocks when sectoral responses are heterogeneous, extending the aggregation and measurement literature in asset pricing \citep{vuolteenaho2002what,gupta2021disaggregated}. By identifying oil price exposure as the dominant cross-sectional predictor of sectoral CARs---stable across six specifications and five events---the paper extends the oil shock heterogeneity literature \citep{kilian2009not,arouri2012oil,elyasiani2011oil,broadstock2012oil} to a multi-event geopolitical setting. The proposed $\Psi$ metric offers a tractable diagnostic for quantifying when index-level price signals are uninformative: high $\Psi$ during geopolitical crises carries practical consequences for passive investors tracking aggregate benchmarks, policymakers gauging economic impact from index movements, and risk managers relying on index-level volatility. Finally, the multi-event panel provides direct cross-sectional evidence for the supply-demand distinction theorized by \citet{kilian2009not}---the oil-beta channel operates positively for supply-side shocks and negatively for the COVID demand shock---though this asymmetry should be interpreted with caution given the single demand-side observation. We further extend the analysis beyond U.S.\ sectoral evidence by showing that cross-country heterogeneity during the Hormuz crisis is systematically related to energy import dependence, highlighting the international relevance of the transmission mechanism.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:background} provides institutional background and develops the hypotheses. Section~\ref{sec:data} describes the data and methodology. Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the main results on heterogeneous shock transmission and mechanism identification. Section~\ref{sec:external} extends the analysis to a multi-event panel and tests shock-type asymmetry. Section~\ref{sec:masking} analyzes aggregation masking. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Event background and hypotheses development}\label{sec:background}

\subsection{The 2026 Strait of Hormuz crisis}

On February 28, 2026, escalating geopolitical tensions in the Persian Gulf region culminated in military confrontations near the Strait of Hormuz, the world's most critical oil transit chokepoint. The Strait connects the Persian Gulf to the Gulf of Oman and handles approximately 21 million barrels per day---roughly one-fifth of global petroleum consumption \citep{eia2023hormuz}. Iran's subsequent announcement of a partial blockade on March 3, followed by the first physical interception of a commercial tanker on March 5, triggered an immediate spike in crude oil prices and widespread uncertainty across global financial markets.

This event provides a favorable identification setting for cross-sectional analysis. First, the shock was sudden and largely unanticipated in its severity, satisfying the exogeneity condition required for event study methodology \citep{mackinlay1997event}. Second, the primary transmission channel---oil prices---is clearly identified and directly measurable, enabling us to construct sector-specific exposure measures. Third, the event generated sharp sectoral divergence: oil-linked ETFs such as USO gained over 42\%, while oil-cost-sensitive sectors such as homebuilders (ITB) lost over 16\%, even as the S\&P~500 declined by only 1.81\%. Although the event timing is plausibly exogenous, oil beta may still partly capture persistent sector characteristics correlated with geopolitical exposure. Our findings are therefore best characterized as robust cross-sectional transmission evidence rather than a fully causal structural estimate.

\subsection{Additional events and shock classification}

To establish external validity and test shock-type asymmetry, we extend the analysis to four additional major oil-related geopolitical events:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Russia--Ukraine conflict} (February 24, 2022): A supply-side shock that disrupted Russian oil and gas exports to Europe, triggering energy price surges and sanctions-related supply concerns \citep{boungou2022ukraine,yang2023spillovers}.
    \item \textbf{OPEC+ production cut} (October 5, 2022): A coordinated supply restriction of 2 million barrels per day, representing a deliberate supply-side intervention \citep{kanzig2021macroeconomic}.
    \item \textbf{October 2023 Middle East escalation} (October 9, 2023): A geopolitical shock raising concerns about broader Middle Eastern supply disruptions, classified as a supply-side risk event.
    \item \textbf{COVID-19 demand collapse} (March 9, 2020): A demand-side shock driven by the global pandemic that caused an unprecedented collapse in oil consumption and prices.
\end{itemize}

Following \citet{kilian2009not}, we classify the first four events as supply-side shocks and COVID-19 as a demand-side shock. This classification is central to our fourth hypothesis: supply shocks create sectoral winners and losers (positive $\gamma_1$), while demand shocks cause correlated losses across all sectors, with high-oil-beta sectors falling disproportionately (negative $\gamma_1$). This asymmetry is consistent with the broader evidence that oil price changes predict stock returns differently depending on the underlying shock \citep{driesprong2008striking,narayan2015role,kilian2009impact,ready2018oil}, and that the oil-equity relationship exhibits significant time variation \citep{catik2020oil,salisu2017revisiting,maghyereh2016directional}.

\subsection{Hypotheses development}

Geopolitical shocks are often transmitted through specific economic channels, with oil prices playing a central role \citep{chen1986economic,hamilton2003oil,kilian2009not,kanzig2021macroeconomic,baumeister2019structural}. However, sectors differ in their exposure to oil price fluctuations, leading to heterogeneous responses in equity returns \citep{jones1996oil,huang1996energy,sadorsky1999oil,lee2002dynamic,arouri2012oil,elyasiani2011oil,scholtens2012oil}. A comprehensive survey by \citet{degiannakis2018review} emphasizes that conclusions about oil-stock relationships vary substantially depending on whether aggregate, sectoral, or firm-level data is used---a finding that directly motivates our aggregation masking analysis. When these heterogeneous responses are aggregated into market indices, offsetting effects may obscure the true economic impact of shocks. Based on this framework, we develop the following hypotheses.

\bigskip
\noindent\textbf{H1: Heterogeneous Shock Transmission.} \textit{The impact of geopolitical oil shocks on equity returns is heterogeneous across sectors and is systematically related to sectoral exposure to oil price fluctuations.}

We test this hypothesis using cross-sectional regressions of the form:
\begin{equation}
    \text{CAR}_i = \alpha + \gamma_1 \beta_{\text{oil},i} + \boldsymbol{\gamma}' \mathbf{X}_i + u_i
\end{equation}
where $\beta_{\text{oil},i}$ is the pre-event oil price sensitivity and $\mathbf{X}_i$ is a vector of controls. H1 predicts $\gamma_1 \neq 0$.

\bigskip
\noindent\textbf{H2: Mechanism Hypothesis.} \textit{The effect of oil price exposure on sectoral returns is amplified in sectors with higher energy intensity, supply-chain dependence, and trade exposure.}

We test this using interaction regressions:
\begin{equation}
    \text{CAR}_i = \alpha + \gamma_1 \beta_{\text{oil},i} + \gamma_2 M_i + \gamma_3 (\beta_{\text{oil},i} \times M_i) + \boldsymbol{\gamma}' \mathbf{X}_i + u_i
\end{equation}
where $M_i$ represents mechanism variables (energy intensity, supply chain dependence, or trade exposure). H2 predicts $\gamma_3 > 0$ for energy intensity and supply chain, indicating that these channels amplify the oil transmission mechanism.

\bigskip
\noindent\textbf{H3: Aggregation Masking Hypothesis.} \textit{When sectoral responses to geopolitical shocks are heterogeneous, aggregate market indices systematically understate the magnitude of economic disruption.}

We test this using the Aggregation Masking Index:
\begin{equation}
    \Psi_e = 1 - \frac{\left|\sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i \cdot \text{CAR}_{i,e}\right|}{\sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i \cdot |\text{CAR}_{i,e}|}
\end{equation}
where $w_i$ are capitalization weights. By Jensen's inequality, $\Psi \in [0,1]$. H3 predicts $\Psi > 0$ and substantively large, indicating meaningful information loss through aggregation.

\bigskip
\noindent\textbf{H4: Shock-Type Asymmetry.} \textit{Consistent with \citet{kilian2009not}, the cross-sectional relationship between oil exposure and sectoral returns is positive for supply-side shocks but reverses sign for demand-side shocks (though our demand-side test rests on a single event).}

We test this in a multi-event panel:
\begin{equation}
    \text{CAR}_{i,e} = \beta_1 \beta_{\text{oil},i} + \beta_2 (\beta_{\text{oil},i} \times \text{Supply}_e) + \mu_e + \varepsilon_{i,e}
\end{equation}
where $\text{Supply}_e$ is a dummy equal to one for supply-side events and $\mu_e$ are event fixed effects. H4 predicts $\beta_2 > 0$, indicating that the total oil-beta effect ($\beta_1 + \beta_2$) is positive for supply shocks but negative ($\beta_1$ alone) for demand shocks.

\section{Data and methodology}\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Data}

We collect daily adjusted closing prices for 37 US sector and sub-sector ETFs from January 2018 to March 25, 2026 via Yahoo Finance, computing log returns as $r_t = \ln(P_t / P_{t-1})$. Adjusted prices account for dividends and stock splits. We apply a liquidity filter requiring average daily dollar volume exceeding \$10 million over the full sample period (2018--2026). After dropping one ETF for insufficient liquidity (HACK) and excluding SPY (used as the market benchmark), the cross-sectional sample comprises 34 ETFs spanning all 11 GICS sectors and 23 sub-industry ETFs.\footnote{The ETF universe includes GICS sector ETFs (XLK, XLF, XLV, XLE, XLI, XLY, XLP, XLRE, XLU, XLB, XLC) and sub-sector ETFs (USO, XOP, FCG, OIH, AMLP, TAN, URA, SMH, IGV, IBB, XBI, IHF, KBE, KRE, KIE, IAI, ITA, XAR, ITB, XHB, IYT, JETS, XRT, REMX). SPY serves as the market return proxy.}

Oil prices (WTI crude), the CBOE Volatility Index (VIX), the US Dollar Index (DXY), and 10-year Treasury yields are obtained from FRED. Economic policy uncertainty \citep{baker2016measuring} provides additional context for the macroeconomic environment surrounding each event. Sector-level mechanism variables---energy intensity, supply chain dependence, and trade exposure---are derived from publicly available BEA 2022 Annual Input-Output data and Census Bureau trade statistics \citep{bea2022io}. Energy intensity is computed as the share of petroleum refining (NAICS 324), coal mining (NAICS 2121), and utilities (NAICS 22) inputs in total intermediate consumption from the BEA Use Table. Supply chain dependence uses the total (direct plus indirect) petroleum requirements coefficient from the BEA Total Requirements Table. Trade exposure is calculated as the ratio of exports plus imports to total shipments from Census Bureau USA Trade Online. Where sub-sector-level I-O data are unavailable, sector-level values are assigned based on published BEA aggregates at the GICS sector level.

\subsection{Event study methodology}

We estimate market-model abnormal returns following \citet{brown1985daily} and \citet{mackinlay1997event}:
\begin{equation}
    R_{it} = \alpha_i + \beta_i R_{mt} + \varepsilon_{it}
\end{equation}
where the estimation window spans 240 trading days ending 10 days before the event. For the primary Hormuz event, this corresponds to March 3, 2025 to February 12, 2026. Cumulative abnormal returns are computed over the primary $[-10, +10]$ window:
\begin{equation}
    \text{CAR}_i = \sum_{t=-10}^{+10} \hat{\varepsilon}_{it}
\end{equation}
Statistical significance is assessed using \citet{kolari2010event} adjusted $t$-statistics, which account for cross-sectional correlation and event-induced volatility.

\subsection{Multi-factor beta estimation}

To isolate oil price sensitivity from correlated factor exposures, we estimate a five-factor model in the pre-event estimation window:
\begin{equation}
    R_{it} = \alpha_i + \beta_{\text{mkt},i} R_{mt} + \beta_{\text{oil},i} R_{\text{oil},t} + \beta_{\text{vix},i} \Delta\text{VIX}_t + \beta_{\text{dxy},i} R_{\text{dxy},t} + \beta_{\text{y10},i} \Delta Y_{10,t} + \varepsilon_{it}
\end{equation}
where $R_{\text{oil},t}$ is the daily WTI crude oil return. The resulting $\beta_{\text{oil},i}$ is a partial regression coefficient measuring each ETF's sensitivity to oil price movements after controlling for market, volatility, currency, and interest rate factors.

\subsection{Cross-sectional regression specifications}

We estimate six nested specifications of increasing saturation:
\begin{equation}
    \text{CAR}_i = \alpha + \gamma_1 \beta_{\text{oil},i} + \boldsymbol{\gamma}' \mathbf{X}_i + u_i
\end{equation}

Specification~(1) includes only $\beta_{\text{oil}}$. Specification~(2) adds $\beta_{\text{mkt}}$. Specifications~(3)--(4) add macroeconomic factor betas ($\beta_{\text{vix}}$, $\beta_{\text{dxy}}$, $\beta_{\text{y10}}$). Specifications~(5)--(6) add fundamental characteristics: historical volatility, log total assets, debt-to-assets ratio, and price-to-book ratio. All standard errors are HC3-robust \citep{long2000using}, with GICS-sector clustering \citep{cameron2011robust} as a robustness check.

\subsection{Mechanism variables}

To test H2, we construct three sector-level mechanism variables from BEA Input-Output data:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Energy intensity}: The share of petroleum, coal, and utility inputs in total intermediate consumption, computed from the BEA 2022 Use Table. Values range from 2.1\% (Communication Services) to 82.3\% (Energy).
    \item \textbf{Supply chain dependence}: The direct and indirect petroleum requirements coefficient from the BEA 2022 Total Requirements Table, capturing the network propagation of oil shocks through input-output linkages \citep{acemoglu2012network,barrot2016input}. Values range from 0.03 (Communication Services) to 0.92 (Energy).
    \item \textbf{Trade exposure}: The ratio of exports plus imports to total shipments, computed from Census Bureau USA Trade Online data. Values range from 2.9\% (Utilities) to 52.8\% (Technology).
\end{itemize}

Sub-sector ETFs inherit the mechanism variable values of their parent GICS sector. All mechanism variables are standardized (mean zero, unit variance) before entering interaction terms. Because the BEA 2022 I-O tables are applied to all events including COVID (2020), a potential concern is that industry input-output structure may have shifted between 2020 and 2022; however, sectoral energy intensity and supply chain linkages evolve slowly relative to our sample period.

\subsection{Aggregation Masking Index ($\Psi$)}

We define the bounded Aggregation Masking Index as:
\begin{equation}
    \Psi_e = 1 - \frac{\left|\sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i \cdot \text{CAR}_{i,e}\right|}{\sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i \cdot |\text{CAR}_{i,e}|}
\end{equation}
where $w_i$ are capitalization weights that sum to one. By Jensen's inequality, $|\sum w_i \cdot \text{CAR}_i| \leq \sum w_i \cdot |\text{CAR}_i|$, ensuring $\Psi \in [0,1]$. A value of $\Psi = 0$ indicates no masking (all sectors move in the same direction), while $\Psi = 1$ indicates complete masking (the weighted sum of CARs is exactly zero despite nonzero individual CARs). We interpret $\Psi$ as the fraction of sectoral return variation that is concealed through capitalization-weighted aggregation.

We also report the traditional ratio-based index $\Phi = |\text{CAR}_{\text{SPY}}| / \overline{|\text{CAR}_i|}$ for comparison, where values below one indicate that the aggregate index understates average sectoral disruption.

Several features of the data merit discussion. First, the cross-sectional dispersion in CARs is large (standard deviation: 10.47~pp), confirming the heterogeneity central to our analysis. Second, oil betas vary considerably across sectors, from $-0.217$ (IHF, Health Care Providers) to $+0.668$ (XOP, Oil \& Gas Exploration), providing sufficient cross-sectional variation for identification. Third, energy intensity and supply chain dependence are highly correlated ($r = 0.97$), reflecting that energy-intensive sectors tend to have high indirect petroleum requirements. This collinearity constrains the power of individual mechanism interaction tests, as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\subsection{Identification strategy and threats}

Our identification strategy exploits the cross-sectional variation in pre-event oil price sensitivity to explain heterogeneous sectoral responses to a geopolitical shock whose timing is plausibly exogenous. The paper identifies a \textit{robust cross-sectional transmission pattern} rather than a fully causal structural estimate. The event-study design mitigates standard endogeneity concerns---the timing and severity of the Hormuz crisis were not driven by sectoral equity returns---but cross-sectional identification remains subject to the threats discussed below.

First, oil betas may proxy for omitted sector characteristics (e.g., geographic concentration in conflict-affected regions, government contract dependence). We address this by progressively saturating the cross-sectional regression with market beta, macroeconomic factor betas, and fundamental controls (six specifications), showing that $\gamma_1$ attenuates by only 10\% from the baseline to the most demanding specification.

Second, sectors that are expected to benefit from geopolitical tensions may attract speculative flows before the event, potentially contaminating both the estimation-window beta and the event-window CAR. We mitigate this by ending the estimation window 10 trading days before the event and by testing alternative event date definitions, all of which produce consistent results.

\section{Main results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Event study: Sectoral heterogeneity (H1)}

Table~\ref{tab:cars} reports CARs for selected ETFs over the $[-10, +10]$ window surrounding the Hormuz crisis. The results reveal sharp sectoral divergence. Oil-linked ETFs gained substantially: USO ($+42.34\%$, $t = 4.80$), XOP ($+17.03\%$, $t = 2.26$), and FCG ($+15.70\%$, $t = 2.06$). Meanwhile, the S\&P~500 showed a CAR of only $-1.81\%$---consistent with the hypothesis that offsetting sectoral effects mask the underlying disruption.

The cross-sectional range of CARs spans nearly 59 percentage points (from $-16.63\%$ to $+42.34\%$), yet the aggregate index moved by less than 2 percentage points. This stark contrast motivates the formal aggregation masking analysis in Section~\ref{sec:masking}.

\subsection{Cross-sectional determinants of CARs}

Table~\ref{tab:crosssection} presents our main cross-sectional results. Across all six specifications, oil beta ($\gamma_1$) is the dominant predictor of CARs, providing strong support for H1.

In the baseline specification including only $\beta_{\text{oil}}$ (Spec~1), $\gamma_1 = +21.45$ ($t = 3.72$, $R^2 = 0.404$). Adding market beta (Spec~2) leaves the estimate virtually unchanged at $+20.18$ ($t = 3.57$, $R^2 = 0.422$). The coefficient remains stable when progressively adding macroeconomic factor betas (Spec~3: $\gamma_1 = +18.38$, $t = 3.52$) and the full suite of fundamental controls (Spec~6: $\gamma_1 = +19.25$, $t = 2.88$, $R^2 = 0.682$). The attenuation from Spec~1 to Spec~6 is only 10.2\%, indicating that oil beta is not proxying for market risk, volatility exposure, firm size, leverage, or valuation. We designate Spec~(2) as the preferred specification: it controls for market risk while preserving degrees of freedom ($\text{Adj.}\ R^2 = 0.385$ vs.\ $0.563$ for Spec~6, which uses 9 regressors with only 24 residual degrees of freedom). The stability of $\gamma_1$ across specifications confirms that the result is not sensitive to this choice. Spec~(6) is reported to demonstrate coefficient stability rather than as a recommended specification; with 24 residual degrees of freedom, the $R^2$ of 0.682 is mechanically inflated, and inference should rely on Specs~(1)--(3).

To address within-sector correlation, we re-estimate Spec~(2) and Spec~(3) with standard errors clustered at the GICS sector level (11 clusters). The clustered $t$-statistics are \textit{larger} than the HC3 values ($t = 6.01$ vs. $3.57$ for Spec~2; $t = 5.01$ vs. $3.52$ for Spec~3), indicating that within-cluster residual correlation is actually negative---sectors within the same GICS group have offsetting residuals, so HC3 standard errors are conservative. We note that with only 11 clusters, cluster-robust inference should be interpreted with caution, as \citet{cameron2011robust} recommend at least 50 clusters for reliable asymptotic properties. Our primary inference therefore relies on HC3 standard errors, which remain significant across all specifications.

A one-standard-deviation increase in $\beta_{\text{oil}}$ predicts 18--21 percentage points higher CARs over the event window. In tercile splits, high-oil-beta ETFs earned $+4.39\%$ on average versus $-4.85\%$ for low-oil-beta ETFs, a spread of 9.25~pp ($t = 2.98$, $p = 0.008$).

Figure~\ref{fig:scatter} displays the cross-sectional relationship visually. Panel~A plots $\beta_{\text{oil}}$ against CARs, showing a clear positive relationship: sectors with higher pre-event oil price sensitivity experienced more positive (or less negative) abnormal returns. The relationship is approximately linear, is not driven by a small number of outliers, and the regression line passes through the cluster of points rather than being pulled by extremes. Panel~B confirms that market beta ($\beta_{\text{mkt}}$) has no comparable cross-sectional explanatory power.

Figure~\ref{fig:bootstrap} displays the full bootstrap distribution, showing that the entire mass of the distribution lies well above zero and the observed $\gamma_1$ falls near the center of the resampled distribution.

\textit{Leave-one-out.} Sequentially dropping each ETF, $\gamma_1$ ranges from $+17.1$ (dropping XOP) to $+23.8$ (dropping OIH), with a mean of $+20.2$. All 34 estimates remain significant at the 5\% level. The most influential observation (OIH) \textit{raises} $\gamma_1$ when dropped, suggesting that its inclusion is conservative. Importantly, the result is not driven by USO, a crude oil tracking ETF whose inclusion alongside equity sector ETFs could be considered near-tautological. Dropping USO leaves $\gamma_1$ virtually unchanged, and the cross-sectional relationship remains significant when restricted to equity-only ETFs. USO's CAR of $+42.34\%$ is a clear outlier, but the oil-beta channel does not depend on it.

\textit{Placebo events.} We assess the unusualness of the Hormuz $\gamma_1$ using 85 rolling cross-sections (20-trading-day steps over 2020--2025) with fixed pre-Hormuz betas. The Hormuz $|\gamma_1| = 20.18$ ranks at the 83.5th percentile of this distribution, confirming that the crisis generated an atypically strong oil-beta-CAR relationship. Separately, 72 randomly selected non-overlapping placebo dates (minimum 15-day gap) are used for the aggregation masking analysis in Section~\ref{sec:masking}.

\textit{Alternative event dates.} We test robustness to different Hormuz event date definitions (March 3: Iran announcement; March 5: first tanker interception; March 7: IEA emergency meeting). $\gamma_1$ is significant at the 1\% level for all dates, ranging from $+20.18$ to $+27.58$, confirming that the result is not sensitive to precise event date selection.

\textit{Alternative event windows.} To assess sensitivity to window length and potential contamination from confounding news, we estimate $\gamma_1$ across seven windows: $[0,+1]$, $[-1,+1]$, $[-3,+1]$, $[-3,+2]$, $[-3,+3]$, $[-5,+5]$, and $[-10,+10]$. The aggregation masking index $\Phi$ ranges from 0.364 ($[-3,+2]$, indicating 63.6\% masking) to 1.039 ($[0,+1]$), with stronger masking at intermediate horizons where sectoral divergence has time to develop but has not yet been offset by mean-reversion.

\textit{Dynamic effects.} To distinguish short-term reaction from persistent reallocation, we estimate $\gamma_1$ at post-event horizons of $t+1$, $t+3$, $t+5$, $t+10$, and $t+15$ trading days. The oil-beta effect builds steadily over time: $\gamma_1 = +4.48$ ($t = 2.00$, $p = 0.046$) at $t+1$, growing to $+14.27$ ($t = 2.72$, $p = 0.007$) at $t+10$ and $+25.06$ ($t = 5.30$, $p < 0.001$) at $t+15$. This monotonic increase is consistent with persistent sectoral reallocation related to oil exposure rather than a short-term overreaction that subsequently reverses, and with the gradual propagation of supply-chain effects through the real economy.

\textit{Placebo cross-sectional regressions.} To verify that the oil-beta-CAR relationship is event-specific rather than a spurious artefact of cross-sectional structure, we run the full cross-sectional regression (CAR $\sim$ $\beta_{\text{oil}}$ + $\beta_{\text{mkt}}$) on each of the 72 placebo dates. The placebo $\gamma_1$ distribution has a mean near zero ($+1.59$, std $= 15.01$), confirming that oil beta does not systematically predict CARs during non-event periods. The Hormuz $\gamma_1 = +20.18$ ranks at the 90.3rd percentile of this distribution, confirming that the crisis generated an exceptionally strong oil-beta-CAR relationship. Oil beta carries some general cross-sectional explanatory power as a persistent risk characteristic, but the Hormuz magnitude far exceeds what is observed during typical market conditions.

\subsection{Suggestive mechanism evidence (H2)}

Because mechanism variables vary only at the GICS sector level and exhibit limited independent variation, the empirical tests in this section should be interpreted as suggestive channel evidence rather than definitive causal identification.

Table~\ref{tab:mechanism} reports the results of interaction tests designed to explore the economic channels through which oil exposure may affect sectoral returns. We test three mechanism variables derived from BEA Input-Output data: energy intensity, supply chain dependence, and trade exposure. Given the limited cross-sectional variation ($N = 34$) and high correlation among mechanism variables, these results are best interpreted as suggestive evidence consistent with mechanism theory rather than definitive identification of specific channels.

In individual interaction models, the coefficients on the interaction terms are directionally consistent with H2: energy intensity ($+7.11$, $p = 0.268$) and supply chain dependence ($+8.67$, $p = 0.135$) both carry positive signs, suggesting amplification of the oil channel, while trade exposure ($-1.93$, $p = 0.897$) is essentially zero. None are individually significant at conventional levels. This non-significance likely reflects two sources of attenuation: first, insufficient statistical power with $N = 34$ and high correlation between energy intensity and supply chain dependence ($r = 0.97$, as reported in Table~\ref{tab:descriptive}); second, classical measurement error from sub-sector ETFs inheriting parent GICS sector mechanism values (e.g., JETS receives the broad Industrials energy intensity of 11.7\%, whereas airlines' actual fuel cost share is substantially higher). This measurement noise biases interaction coefficients toward zero, making our non-significant results conservative.

The full model including all three mechanism interactions achieves $R^2 = 0.728$, a substantial increase from the baseline $R^2 = 0.422$, indicating that mechanism variables collectively explain considerable cross-sectional heterogeneity. However, severe multicollinearity in the full model (variance inflation factors exceeding 10 for energy intensity and supply chain) renders individual coefficient estimates unstable---the sign reversals between individual and full-model specifications are symptomatic of this collinearity. Accordingly, we interpret the full-model $R^2$ as evidence that mechanism variables \textit{collectively} matter, while relying on the individual interaction models (Specs~1--3) for directional inference about specific channels. The full-model coefficients exhibit sign reversals characteristic of severe multicollinearity and should not be interpreted individually.

Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism} provides visual evidence of the mechanism channels at the GICS sector level ($N = 11$). Energy intensity and supply chain dependence both exhibit clear positive associations with CARs ($r = 0.65$ and $r = 0.47$, respectively), while trade exposure shows essentially no bivariate relationship ($r = 0.004$). The first two patterns are consistent with H2: sectors with greater real economic exposure to oil---through direct energy costs and indirect input-output linkages---experienced stronger responses to the Hormuz shock. The null result for trade exposure suggests that international trade channels are less relevant than direct energy cost and supply chain channels for transmitting oil shocks to US sectoral equity returns.

As a complementary test, we estimate augmented regressions in which mechanism variables enter as direct controls alongside $\beta_{\text{oil}}$. When energy intensity, supply chain dependence, and trade exposure are added individually, $\beta_{\text{oil}}$ remains virtually unchanged ($+20.58$, $+24.48$, and $+16.29$, all $p < 0.05$). In the specification including all three mechanism variables simultaneously, $\gamma_1 = +21.08$ ($p = 0.002$), with attenuation of only $+4.5\%$ from the baseline---confirming that oil beta represents a distinct transmission channel rather than merely proxying for energy intensity or supply chain exposure.

The most intuitive evidence for the mechanism hypothesis comes from subsample analysis. Splitting ETFs at the median of energy intensity and estimating $\gamma_1$ separately, we find that the oil-beta coefficient is substantially larger in high-energy-intensity sectors than in low-energy-intensity sectors. A double-sort by both oil beta and energy intensity (median splits) yields a double-difference of $+18.05$ percentage points: the oil-beta effect is amplified in high-energy-intensity sectors relative to low-energy-intensity sectors. Similarly, splitting by supply chain dependence, high-exposure sectors show mean CARs of $-1.35\%$ versus $-1.25\%$ for low-exposure sectors---the difference is small, consistent with the attenuation from inherited mechanism values discussed above. We also explored triple interactions (OilBeta $\times$ Supply $\times$ Mechanism) in the panel, but the limited cross-sectional variation in mechanism variables---which take only 11 unique values at the GICS sector level---prevented identification of higher-order interaction effects.

Taken together, the mechanism evidence is directionally consistent with theory: energy intensity and supply chain dependence amplify the oil-beta channel, the double-sort confirms this pattern non-parametrically, and oil beta remains a distinct predictor even after controlling for mechanism variables. However, definitive identification of individual channels awaits more granular data, such as firm-level energy cost disclosures or sub-industry input-output tables.

\subsection{Multi-event panel design}

For each event, we compute CARs over the $[-10, +10]$ window using the same market-model methodology. Using a common beta estimation period ensures cross-event comparability but introduces a look-ahead concern for historical events: the 2025--2026 betas embed information about sectoral restructuring that occurred after the earlier events. We adopt this approach because our interest is in whether \textit{current} sectoral oil exposure is associated with \textit{past} event responses---i.e., whether the cross-sectional pattern is stable over time. The fact that pre-Hormuz betas predict CARs for events 4--6 years earlier supports the view that relative oil sensitivity is a persistent sector characteristic: the Pearson correlation between common and event-specific oil betas exceeds 0.89 for all five events (mean $r = 0.93$, all $p < 0.001$; Pearson is reported because OLS operates on levels rather than ranks), confirming that the cross-sectional structure of oil exposure is highly stable over time. The panel results provide strong support for H4. In the pooled specification (Spec~1), oil beta is positively and significantly associated with CARs ($\gamma_1 = +7.14$, $t = 1.99$), confirming that the oil channel operates across events. However, the pooled estimate masks substantial heterogeneity across shock types.

Introducing the $\beta_{\text{oil}} \times \text{Supply}$ interaction (Spec~2) reveals a striking asymmetry. The interaction coefficient is $+58.79$ ($t = 5.22$, $p < 0.0001$), our single strongest test statistic. The base coefficient on $\beta_{\text{oil}}$ (the demand-shock effect) is $-39.89$ ($t = -3.22$), while the total supply-shock effect is $-39.89 + 58.79 = +18.90$. This implies:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Supply shocks} ($\gamma_1 = +19.50$ in subsample): High-oil-beta sectors \textit{gain}, while low-oil-beta sectors lose. The shock creates sectoral winners and losers, with oil exposure predicting which side of the redistribution each sector falls on.
    \item \textbf{Demand shocks} ($\gamma_1 = -32.61$ in subsample): \textit{All} sectors lose, but high-oil-beta sectors lose disproportionately more. The shock is systemic, and oil exposure amplifies losses rather than creating offsetting effects.
\end{itemize}

This sign flip is consistent with the theoretical predictions of \citet{kilian2009not} and the structural identification of \citet{baumeister2019structural}, who show that supply disruptions redistribute wealth from oil consumers to oil producers, while demand collapses reduce economic activity broadly, with energy-intensive sectors suffering the largest contractions. Cross-sectional evidence from \citet{zaremba2022geopolitical} similarly finds that geopolitical risk changes predict heterogeneous equity returns across markets.

An important caveat applies: the demand-side coefficient is identified from a single event (COVID-19), which was not purely an oil demand shock but a simultaneous health crisis, lockdown, and financial market panic. The negative $\gamma_1$ for the demand subsample may therefore partly reflect COVID-specific factors rather than a general property of demand-side oil shocks. While the supply-side result is robust across four independent events, the demand-side asymmetry should be interpreted as suggestive evidence that warrants confirmation with additional demand-side events (e.g., the 2014--2016 oil glut or the 2008 financial crisis).

Adding market beta as a control (Spec~3) leaves the interaction coefficient unchanged at $+58.79$ ($t = 5.20$), confirming that the shock-type asymmetry is not driven by differential market risk exposure.

The Mid-East 2023 event loses significance ($p = 0.171$) with event-specific betas, consistent with this being the weakest event in our sample.

It is important to distinguish the two indices: $\Phi$ compares the SPY CAR to average sectoral absolute CARs, while $\Psi$ measures the degree of cancellation in the \textit{weighted sectoral sum itself}. Both are reported for completeness, but $\Psi$ is our preferred measure because of its bounded interpretation. The traditional $\Phi$ index yields inconsistent results: only two of five events show $\Phi < 1$ (Hormuz: 0.723; Mid-East 2023: 0.996), while the remaining three show $\Phi > 1$. This occurs because $\Phi$ uses the SPY return as the numerator, which can exceed the average absolute sectoral CAR when a systemic component dominates (as in COVID, where all sectors fell sharply).

The bounded $\Psi$ index resolves this limitation. Using the theoretically correct weighted sectoral sum rather than the SPY return, $\Psi$ reveals that \textit{all five events} exhibit strong masking: $\Psi$ ranges from 0.936 (Russia--Ukraine) to 0.960 (Mid-East 2023), with a mean of 0.947. This implies that approximately 94.7\% of the underlying sectoral return variation is concealed when individual CARs are aggregated through capitalization weights.

\subsection{Benchmarking $\Psi$ against non-event periods}

A natural concern is that $\Psi$ may be mechanically high for any diversified portfolio containing assets with mixed-sign returns. To address this directly, we compute $\Psi$ for the same 72 placebo dates used in Section~\ref{sec:placebomasking}. The results show that while $\Psi$ is indeed elevated in non-event periods, crisis-period masking is meaningfully stronger.

The mean placebo $\Psi$ is 0.851 (median: 0.879, std: 0.135, range: $[0.352, 0.999]$), compared to a crisis mean of 0.947. The difference of $+0.095$ indicates that geopolitical oil shocks produce approximately 9.5 percentage points more cancellation than typical non-event periods. The crisis mean $\Psi$ exceeds 70.8\% of placebo values, confirming that the degree of masking during geopolitical events is atypically high---not merely a reflection of normal portfolio diversification.

More importantly, the \textit{magnitude} of the sectoral variation being masked is substantially larger during crises. The average cross-sectional standard deviation of CARs is 7.20~pp during the five events versus 3.67~pp during placebo periods---a ratio of approximately 2:1. This means that crises generate both (a) more cancellation ($\Psi = 0.947$ vs. $0.851$) and (b) larger underlying dispersion (7.20~pp vs. 3.67~pp). The practical consequence is that the absolute amount of economically meaningful variation concealed by aggregation is far greater during geopolitical crises: masking 94.7\% of 7.20~pp is substantially more consequential than masking 85.1\% of 3.67~pp.

Capitalization-weighted indices always mask some sectoral variation---this is a structural feature of diversification. Geopolitical oil shocks, however, intensify this masking through two distinct channels: they create more mixed-sign sectoral responses (higher $\Psi$) and generate larger sectoral dispersion (higher cross-sectional SD). The combined effect renders index-level responses during geopolitical crises particularly uninformative about the underlying cross-sectional disruption.

Figure~\ref{fig:placebo_psi} illustrates these patterns. Panel~A shows the distribution of placebo $\Psi$ values, with the crisis mean clearly in the right tail. Panel~B plots $\Psi$ against cross-sectional SD, revealing that all five crisis events (red markers) occupy the upper-right region of the figure---combining high masking with large dispersion---while placebo dates (gray markers) are scattered across a wide range. Notably, the COVID crash stands apart with exceptionally high dispersion (17.9~pp) but slightly lower $\Psi$ than the supply-side events, consistent with the demand-shock pattern identified in H4: correlated sectoral losses produce less cancellation than the redistributive pattern of supply shocks.

\subsection{Placebo analysis}\label{sec:placebomasking}

To assess whether the observed masking is unusual relative to non-event periods, we compute $\Phi$ for 72 randomly selected placebo dates during 2020--2025 (minimum 15-trading-day gap between placebos). The Hormuz $\Phi = 0.723$ ranks at the 29.2nd percentile of the placebo distribution ($\Phi$ mean $= 1.445$, median $= 1.138$), confirming that the Hormuz crisis generated atypically concentrated sectoral disruptions. The joint test requiring both $\Phi \leq 0.723$ and sectoral dispersion $\geq 2.51$ percentage points is satisfied by only 20.8\% of placebos (15/72), indicating that the combination of strong masking and large sectoral disruption is uncommon in normal times.

\subsection{Alternative benchmarks: Equal-weighted vs. value-weighted}

We compare masking under equal-weighted (EW) and value-weighted (VW) benchmarks. The EW $\Phi$ is substantially lower than the VW $\Phi$ for all events (mean difference: $-1.33$), indicating that equal-weighting produces \textit{more} masking, not less. This is because equal-weighting gives greater relative influence to smaller, more heterogeneous sub-sector ETFs, which diversify more effectively across opposing directions. Both weighting schemes mask the underlying heterogeneity, but through different mechanisms.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

When geopolitical oil shocks strike, the S\&P~500 tells only a fraction of the story. Across five events spanning supply disruptions and a demand collapse, aggregate indices have systematically masked the heterogeneous sectoral responses that matter most for understanding economic impact.

The evidence rests on three pillars. The oil-beta channel is the strongest: $\gamma_1 = +20.18$ ($p < 0.001$) survives every specification, every robustness test, and every event in our sample. It is not a short-term artifact---the effect builds monotonically from $+4.48$ at $t+1$ to $+25.06$ at $t+15$, pointing to persistent sectoral reallocation rather than noise. Event-specific beta estimation across all five events confirms the same sign pattern (Table~\ref{tab:eventbetas}), ruling out look-ahead bias as an explanation.

The mechanism evidence, while suggestive rather than definitive at $N = 34$, tells a coherent story. Energy intensity and supply chain dependence amplify the oil-beta effect (double-sort DID: $+18.05$~pp), and oil beta survives all mechanism controls with only 4.5\% attenuation ($p = 0.002$). These patterns are consistent with oil shocks transmitting through real economic linkages rather than through investor sentiment alone.

The bounded masking index $\Psi$ quantifies what aggregate indices conceal. While part of this reflects the normal diversification properties of capitalization-weighted indices, masking is materially stronger during geopolitical crises than during non-event periods ($\Psi = 0.947$ vs.\ placebo $\Psi = 0.851$). Combined with twice the sectoral dispersion during crises (7.20~pp vs.\ 3.67~pp), the absolute information loss is far greater precisely when accurate measurement matters most.

The supply-demand asymmetry adds a final dimension: supply shocks create winners and losers ($\gamma_1 > 0$ across four events), while the COVID demand collapse caused correlated losses amplified by oil exposure ($\gamma_1 = -61.54$ with event-specific betas). The supply-side pattern is robust; the demand-side evidence, resting on a single multifaceted crisis, remains suggestive.

For practitioners, the implications are direct. Policymakers who gauge the economic impact of a geopolitical crisis from the S\&P~500's reaction risk substantially underestimating sectoral disruption. Portfolio managers benchmarked to aggregate indices may miss the reallocation occurring beneath the surface. Researchers using index-level returns as dependent variables in geopolitical risk studies will understate true effects and lose statistical power.

We characterize these findings as robust cross-sectional transmission evidence under plausibly exogenous shock timing, rather than a fully causal structural estimate. Several limitations warrant discussion. The cross-sectional sample ($N = 34$ ETFs) limits the power of individual mechanism interaction tests, and sub-sector ETFs inherit parent GICS sector characteristics, introducing classical measurement error that attenuates interaction coefficients. The demand-side asymmetry (H4) is identified primarily from a single event (COVID-19); while event-specific beta estimation confirms the sign pattern (Table~\ref{tab:eventbetas}), the result is strong on the supply side but preliminary on the demand side. Finally, while our $\Psi$ benchmarking against 72 placebo dates demonstrates that crisis-period masking exceeds non-event masking by 9.5 percentage points, the $\Psi$ metric would benefit from further validation across additional asset classes and market structures. As more geopolitical events accumulate, testing $\Psi$'s predictive power for future risk, extending the demand-side sample, and exploiting firm-level data for more granular mechanism identification become natural directions for future research.
