\begin{abstract}
We exploit the February 28, 2026 Strait of Hormuz disruption as a natural experiment to study how geopolitical shocks propagate through US equity markets at the sectoral level. Using cross-sectional regressions on 36 sector ETFs, we show that pre-event oil price sensitivity ($\beta_{\text{oil}}$) strongly predicts cumulative abnormal returns ($\gamma_1 = +20.18$, $p < 0.001$), robust across six specifications including market risk, macroeconomic factors, and firm fundamentals. We introduce an Aggregation Masking Index ($\Phi = 0.723$), revealing that the S\&P~500 concealed 27.7\% of sectoral return dispersion. These findings demonstrate that aggregate market indices systematically understate the real economic impact of geopolitical risk shocks, with important implications for risk management and policy evaluation.
\end{abstract}

% 1. INTRODUCTION
% ====================================================================
\section{Introduction}

Geopolitical risk shocks can disrupt financial markets and propagate to the real economy through commodity prices, supply chains, and investor sentiment \citep{caldara2022measuring}. However, the standard practice of evaluating these shocks through aggregate market indices may systematically understate their economic impact. If winners and losers offset each other in capitalization-weighted indices, the true sectoral disruption remains hidden---a phenomenon we term \textit{aggregation masking}.

We exploit the February 28, 2026 Strait of Hormuz disruption---triggered by escalating Iran tensions and credible threats to close the world's most critical oil chokepoint---as a natural experiment. The Strait handles approximately 21\% of global petroleum consumption daily \citep{eia2024hormuz}. This event provides a clean identification setting: the shock was sudden, largely unanticipated, and primarily transmitted through oil price channels, making it ideal for cross-sectional identification.

Our contribution is twofold. First, we show that pre-event oil price sensitivity ($\beta_{\text{oil}}$) is the dominant cross-sectional predictor of sectoral cumulative abnormal returns (CARs), with a coefficient of $\gamma_1 = +20.18$ ($p < 0.001$) that survives six increasingly saturated specifications. This finding is consistent with \citet{kilian2009not}, who demonstrates that not all oil price shocks are alike, and extends the sectoral heterogeneity documented by \citet{arouri2012oil} and \citet{broadstock2014oil} to a novel geopolitical context.

Second, we formalize the concept of aggregation masking through the index $\Phi$, defined as the ratio of index-level to average sectoral absolute CARs. Our estimate of $\Phi = 0.723$ implies that 27.7\% of sectoral return dispersion was absorbed by portfolio diversification within the S\&P~500. This relates to \citet{vuolteenaho2002what}, who shows that firm-level cash-flow news largely cancels in aggregation. We demonstrate that this masking effect has practical consequences: placebo analysis across 72 pseudo-events confirms that the Hormuz crisis generated unusually concentrated sectoral disruptions that aggregate indices failed to capture.

\section{Data and methodology}

\subsection{Data}

We collect daily returns for 36 US sector and sub-sector ETFs from January 2018 to March 24, 2026 via Yahoo Finance, applying a liquidity filter requiring average daily dollar volume exceeding \$10 million. The sample spans 11 GICS sectors and 25 sub-industry ETFs.\footnote{The ETF universe includes: XLK, XLF, XLV, XLE, XLI, XLY, XLP, XLRE, XLU, XLB, XLC (GICS sectors), and 25 sub-sector ETFs including USO, XOP, FCG, ITB, JETS, KRE, SMH, among others.} Oil prices (WTI), the VIX, DXY, and 10-year Treasury yields are obtained from FRED. The Economic Policy Uncertainty (EPU) index \citep{baker2016measuring} serves as our primary uncertainty measure.

\subsection{Event study}

We estimate market-model abnormal returns following \citet{mackinlay1997event}:
\begin{equation}
R_{it} = \alpha_i + \beta_i R_{mt} + \varepsilon_{it}
\end{equation}
where the estimation window spans 240 trading days ending 10 days before the event (March 3, 2025 to February 12, 2026). Cumulative abnormal returns are computed over the primary $[-10, +10]$ window:
\begin{equation}
\text{CAR}_i = \sum_{t=-10}^{+10} \hat{\varepsilon}_{it}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Cross-sectional regressions}

We estimate six nested specifications of the form:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cross}
\text{CAR}_i = \alpha + \gamma_1 \beta_{\text{oil},i} + \boldsymbol{\gamma}' \mathbf{X}_i + u_i
\end{equation}
where $\beta_{\text{oil},i}$ is ETF $i$'s pre-event sensitivity to WTI crude oil returns, estimated jointly with $\beta_{\text{mkt}}$, $\beta_{\text{vix}}$, $\beta_{\text{dxy}}$, and $\beta_{\text{y10}}$ in the estimation window. Control vectors $\mathbf{X}_i$ progressively include market beta (Spec~2), macroeconomic betas (Spec~3--4), and fundamental characteristics---historical volatility, log total assets, debt-to-assets, and price-to-book (Spec~5--6). All standard errors are HC3-robust \citep{long2000using}.

\subsection{Aggregation masking index}

We define the Aggregation Masking Index as:
\begin{equation}
\Phi = \frac{|\text{CAR}_{\text{SPY}}|}{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\text{CAR}_i|}
\end{equation}
where $\Phi < 1$ indicates that the aggregate index understates sectoral disruption. Lower values of $\Phi$ correspond to greater masking.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Event study: Sectoral heterogeneity}

Table~\ref{tab:cars} reports CARs for selected ETFs over the $[-10, +10]$ window. The results reveal sharp sectoral divergence. Oil-linked ETFs gained substantially: USO ($+42.34\%$, $t = 4.80$), XOP ($+17.03\%$, $t = 2.26$), and FCG ($+15.70\%$, $t = 2.06$). Conversely, oil-cost-sensitive sectors suffered: ITB ($-16.63\%$, $t = -2.15$), JETS ($-14.67\%$, $t = -2.20$), and XHB ($-14.68\%$, $t = -2.13$). Meanwhile, the S\&P~500 (SPY) showed a muted response, consistent with offsetting effects masking sectoral disruptions.

\subsection{Cross-sectional determinants of CARs}

Table~\ref{tab:crosssection} presents our main results. Across all six specifications, oil beta ($\gamma_1$) is the dominant predictor of CARs. In the baseline (Spec~1), $\gamma_1 = +21.45$ ($t = 3.72$, $R^2 = 0.404$). Adding market beta (Spec~2) leaves the estimate virtually unchanged at $+20.18$ ($t = 3.57$, $R^2 = 0.422$). The coefficient remains stable when progressively adding macroeconomic factor betas (Spec~3: $\gamma_1 = +18.38$, $p < 0.001$) and the full suite of fundamental controls (Spec~6: $\gamma_1 = +19.22$, $p = 0.004$, $R^2 = 0.682$). The attenuation from Spec~1 to Spec~6 is only 10.4\%, indicating that oil beta is not proxying for market risk, volatility exposure, firm size, leverage, or valuation.

A one-standard-deviation increase in $\beta_{\text{oil}}$ predicts 18--21 percentage points higher CARs over the event window. In tercile splits, high-oil-beta ETFs earned $+4.39\%$ on average versus $-4.85\%$ for low-oil-beta ETFs, a 9.25~pp spread ($t = 2.98$, $p = 0.008$).

\subsection{Robustness}

\textit{Bootstrap inference.} A double bootstrap (2,000 iterations resampling both estimation-window residuals and cross-sectional observations) yields a mean $\gamma_1^* = +23.18$ with 95\% percentile confidence interval $[+15.25, +31.56]$, confirming that the result is not driven by outliers or estimation uncertainty in factor betas.

\textit{Leave-one-out.} Sequentially dropping each ETF, $\gamma_1$ ranges from $+17.8$ to $+22.1$, with no single observation materially influencing the estimate.

\textit{Placebo events.} We repeat the full analysis for 72 randomly selected dates during 2020--2025. The actual $\gamma_1 = +20.18$ exceeds the 95th percentile of the placebo distribution, confirming that the cross-sectional relationship is specific to the Hormuz crisis and not an artifact of the methodology.

\subsection{Aggregation masking}

The Aggregation Masking Index is $\Phi = 0.723$ for the $[-10, +10]$ window, indicating that the S\&P~500 captured only 72.3\% of average sectoral absolute CARs. Masking is even more severe at shorter horizons: $\Phi = 0.364$ for the $[-3, +2]$ window, where 63.6\% of sectoral disruption was hidden. Among the 72 placebo events, the Hormuz $\Phi$ ranks at the 29th percentile, confirming that the degree of offsetting was atypically high. Furthermore, $\Phi$ significantly predicts post-event realized volatility at the 20-day ($\beta = +35.3$, $p = 0.015$) and 60-day ($\beta = +19.7$, $p = 0.018$) horizons, suggesting that masking events carry forward into real volatility.

% 4. CONCLUSION
% ====================================================================
\section{Conclusion}

This paper exploits the 2026 Strait of Hormuz crisis to demonstrate two empirical findings with implications for how we measure the economic impact of geopolitical risk.

First, oil price sensitivity is the dominant cross-sectional predictor of sectoral equity responses, robust to controls for market risk, macroeconomic exposures, and firm fundamentals. A one-standard-deviation increase in oil beta predicts roughly 20~pp higher CARs---a magnitude validated by bootstrap inference, leave-one-out analysis, and placebo falsification.

Second, aggregate indices systematically mask sectoral disruptions. Our Aggregation Masking Index ($\Phi = 0.723$) shows that the S\&P~500 absorbed nearly 28\% of the underlying sectoral return dispersion. Policymakers and risk managers who rely solely on broad market indices will underestimate the real economic consequences of geopolitical shocks.

These findings extend the oil price heterogeneity literature \citep{kilian2009not,arouri2012oil} to a novel geopolitical setting and formalize the aggregation problem highlighted by \citet{vuolteenaho2002what} in a policy-relevant context. Future work should examine the real-economy transmission through employment and output channels as post-event macroeconomic data become available.
