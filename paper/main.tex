\begin{abstract}
Financial economists and practitioners routinely rely on aggregate market indices as summary statistics of economic conditions and the impact of macroeconomic shocks. This paper challenges the assumption that such indices accurately reflect the underlying economic effects of geopolitical oil shocks. Using a multi-event framework spanning five major oil-related shocks---the 2026 Strait of Hormuz crisis, the Russia--Ukraine conflict, OPEC+ production cuts, the Hamas attack, and the COVID-19 demand collapse---we show that pre-event oil price sensitivity ($\beta_{\text{oil}}$) strongly predicts cross-sectional cumulative abnormal returns ($\gamma_1 = +20.18$, $p < 0.0001$). Consistent with \citet{kilian2009not}, the transmission channel operates asymmetrically---positively for supply-side shocks and negatively for demand-side shocks, with a highly significant interaction coefficient of $+58.79$ ($p < 0.0001$). To quantify the information lost through aggregation, we introduce a bounded Aggregation Masking Index ($\Psi$), which reveals that approximately 95\% of sectoral return variation is netted out when individual sector responses are aggregated into a capitalization-weighted index. These findings demonstrate that aggregate indices can substantially misrepresent the true economic impact of geopolitical shocks, with implications for policymakers, investors, and researchers who use market indices to infer macroeconomic conditions.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Financial economists and practitioners routinely rely on aggregate market indices---such as the S\&P~500---as summary statistics of economic conditions and the impact of macroeconomic shocks. This widespread practice implicitly assumes that aggregate indices accurately reflect the underlying economic effects of such shocks. In this paper, we challenge this assumption. We argue that when shocks generate heterogeneous responses across sectors, aggregate indices can systematically misrepresent the true economic impact.

Geopolitical shocks provide a particularly relevant setting in which this issue arises. Such shocks are typically transmitted through specific economic channels---most notably energy prices, supply chains, and trade exposure---affecting industries asymmetrically \citep{kilian2009not,caldara2022measuring,smales2021geopolitical}. As a result, some sectors benefit while others experience substantial losses. When these heterogeneous responses are aggregated through capitalization-weighted indices, positive and negative effects may offset each other, leading to a muted aggregate response. Consequently, aggregate indices may exhibit a form of aggregation bias, obscuring economically meaningful sectoral disruptions.

We study this phenomenon using a multi-event framework centered on five major geopolitical oil shocks: the 2026 Strait of Hormuz crisis, the 2022 Russia--Ukraine conflict, the 2022 OPEC+ production cut, the 2023 Hamas attack, and the 2020 COVID-19 demand collapse. These events span both supply-side and demand-side oil shocks, enabling us to test whether the transmission channel operates differently across shock types---a distinction emphasized by \citet{kilian2009not} and \citet{kilian2009impact}.

We exploit the February 28, 2026 Strait of Hormuz disruption as our primary quasi-natural experiment. The Strait handles approximately 21\% of global petroleum consumption daily \citep{eia2023hormuz}, and the crisis was sudden, largely unanticipated, and primarily transmitted through oil price channels, making it ideal for cross-sectional identification. Using cross-sectional regressions on 34 sector and sub-sector ETFs, we show that pre-event oil price sensitivity ($\beta_{\text{oil}}$) strongly predicts cumulative abnormal returns (CARs), with $\gamma_1 = +20.18$ ($p < 0.0001$) that survives six increasingly saturated specifications including market risk, macroeconomic factors, and firm fundamentals. This finding generalizes across all five events in our panel.

To quantify the extent to which aggregate indices mask these heterogeneous responses, we introduce a novel bounded measure---the Aggregation Masking Index ($\Psi$). Unlike simple ratios of index-level to sectoral returns, $\Psi$ is bounded on $[0,1]$ by Jensen's inequality and captures the degree to which capitalization-weighted aggregation conceals underlying sectoral variation. Empirically, we find $\Psi \approx 0.947$ across all five events, implying that approximately 95\% of the weighted sectoral return dispersion is netted out through capitalization-weighted aggregation. Because diversified portfolios are designed to cancel idiosyncratic variation, a high $\Psi$ partly reflects the normal functioning of index construction. However, the near-invariance of $\Psi$ across events of very different magnitudes and types suggests that geopolitical shocks systematically generate the kind of mixed-sign sectoral responses that diversification eliminates---an important consideration for those who interpret index-level movements as measures of economic impact.

This paper makes four contributions. First, we demonstrate that aggregate market indices systematically underestimate the economic impact of geopolitical shocks when sectoral responses are heterogeneous, contributing to the literature on aggregation and measurement in asset pricing \citep{vuolteenaho2002what,gupta2021disaggregated}. Second, we identify oil price exposure as a key transmission mechanism linking geopolitical shocks to cross-sectional equity returns, extending the oil shock heterogeneity literature \citep{kilian2009not,arouri2012oil,elyasiani2011oil,broadstock2012oil}. Third, we propose $\Psi$ as a tractable and interpretable metric for quantifying aggregation-induced information loss, providing a new tool for measuring the gap between index-level and sector-level responses. Fourth, we provide direct cross-sectional evidence for the supply-demand distinction theorized by \citet{kilian2009not}: the oil-beta channel operates positively for supply-side shocks (four events) and negatively for the demand-side COVID shock, though the asymmetry result should be interpreted with caution given the single demand-side observation.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:background} provides institutional background and develops the hypotheses. Section~\ref{sec:data} describes the data and methodology. Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the main results on heterogeneous shock transmission and mechanism identification. Section~\ref{sec:external} extends the analysis to a multi-event panel and tests shock-type asymmetry. Section~\ref{sec:masking} analyzes aggregation masking. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Event background and hypotheses development}\label{sec:background}

\subsection{The 2026 Strait of Hormuz crisis}

On February 28, 2026, escalating tensions between Iran and a US-Israeli coalition culminated in military strikes near the Strait of Hormuz, the world's most critical oil transit chokepoint. The Strait connects the Persian Gulf to the Gulf of Oman and handles approximately 21 million barrels per day---roughly one-fifth of global petroleum consumption \citep{eia2023hormuz}. Iran's subsequent announcement of a partial blockade on March 3, followed by the first physical interception of a commercial tanker on March 5, triggered an immediate spike in crude oil prices and widespread uncertainty across global financial markets.

This event provides a particularly clean identification setting for several reasons. First, the shock was sudden and largely unanticipated in its severity, satisfying the exogeneity condition required for event study methodology \citep{mackinlay1997event}. Second, the primary transmission channel---oil prices---is clearly identified and directly measurable, enabling us to construct sector-specific exposure measures. Third, the event generated sharp sectoral divergence: oil-linked ETFs such as USO gained over 42\%, while oil-cost-sensitive sectors such as homebuilders (ITB) lost over 16\%, even as the S\&P~500 declined by only 1.81\%.

\subsection{Additional events and shock classification}

To establish external validity and test shock-type asymmetry, we extend the analysis to four additional major oil-related geopolitical events:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Russia--Ukraine conflict} (February 24, 2022): A supply-side shock that disrupted Russian oil and gas exports to Europe, triggering energy price surges and sanctions-related supply concerns.
    \item \textbf{OPEC+ production cut} (October 5, 2022): A coordinated supply restriction of 2 million barrels per day, representing a deliberate supply-side intervention.
    \item \textbf{Hamas attack} (October 9, 2023): A geopolitical shock raising concerns about broader Middle Eastern supply disruptions, classified as a supply-side risk event.
    \item \textbf{COVID-19 demand collapse} (March 9, 2020): A demand-side shock driven by the global pandemic that caused an unprecedented collapse in oil consumption and prices.
\end{itemize}

Following \citet{kilian2009not}, we classify the first four events as supply-side shocks and COVID-19 as a demand-side shock. This classification is central to our fourth hypothesis: supply shocks create sectoral winners and losers (positive $\gamma_1$), while demand shocks cause correlated losses across all sectors, with high-oil-beta sectors falling disproportionately (negative $\gamma_1$). This asymmetry is consistent with the broader evidence that oil price changes predict stock returns differently depending on the underlying shock \citep{driesprong2008striking,narayan2015role,kilian2009impact,ready2018oil}, and that the oil-equity relationship exhibits significant time variation \citep{catik2020oil}.

\subsection{Hypotheses development}

Geopolitical shocks are often transmitted through specific economic channels, with oil prices playing a central role \citep{hamilton2003oil,kilian2009not,baumeister2019structural}. However, sectors differ in their exposure to oil price fluctuations, leading to heterogeneous responses in equity returns \citep{jones1996oil,sadorsky1999oil,arouri2012oil,elyasiani2011oil,scholtens2012oil}. A comprehensive survey by \citet{degiannakis2018review} emphasizes that conclusions about oil-stock relationships vary substantially depending on whether aggregate, sectoral, or firm-level data is used---a finding that directly motivates our aggregation masking analysis. When these heterogeneous responses are aggregated into market indices, offsetting effects may obscure the true economic impact of shocks. Based on this framework, we develop the following hypotheses.

\bigskip
\noindent\textbf{H1: Heterogeneous Shock Transmission.} \textit{The impact of geopolitical oil shocks on equity returns is heterogeneous across sectors and is systematically related to sectoral exposure to oil price fluctuations.}

We test this hypothesis using cross-sectional regressions of the form:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:h1}
    \text{CAR}_i = \alpha + \gamma_1 \beta_{\text{oil},i} + \boldsymbol{\gamma}' \mathbf{X}_i + u_i
\end{equation}
where $\beta_{\text{oil},i}$ is the pre-event oil price sensitivity and $\mathbf{X}_i$ is a vector of controls. H1 predicts $\gamma_1 \neq 0$.

\bigskip
\noindent\textbf{H2: Mechanism Hypothesis.} \textit{The effect of oil price exposure on sectoral returns is amplified in sectors with higher energy intensity, supply-chain dependence, and trade exposure.}

We test this using interaction regressions:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:h2}
    \text{CAR}_i = \alpha + \gamma_1 \beta_{\text{oil},i} + \gamma_2 M_i + \gamma_3 (\beta_{\text{oil},i} \times M_i) + \boldsymbol{\gamma}' \mathbf{X}_i + u_i
\end{equation}
where $M_i$ represents mechanism variables (energy intensity, supply chain dependence, or trade exposure). H2 predicts $\gamma_3 > 0$ for energy intensity and supply chain, indicating that these channels amplify the oil transmission mechanism.

\bigskip
\noindent\textbf{H3: Aggregation Masking Hypothesis.} \textit{When sectoral responses to geopolitical shocks are heterogeneous, aggregate market indices systematically understate the magnitude of economic disruption.}

We test this using the Aggregation Masking Index:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:h3}
    \Psi_e = 1 - \frac{\left|\sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i \cdot \text{CAR}_{i,e}\right|}{\sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i \cdot |\text{CAR}_{i,e}|}
\end{equation}
where $w_i$ are capitalization weights. By Jensen's inequality, $\Psi \in [0,1]$. H3 predicts $\Psi > 0$ and substantively large, indicating meaningful information loss through aggregation.

\bigskip
\noindent\textbf{H4: Shock-Type Asymmetry.} \textit{The oil-beta transmission channel operates asymmetrically across shock types: positively for supply-side shocks and negatively for demand-side shocks.}

We test this in a multi-event panel:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:h4}
    \text{CAR}_{i,e} = \beta_1 \beta_{\text{oil},i} + \beta_2 (\beta_{\text{oil},i} \times \text{Supply}_e) + \mu_e + \varepsilon_{i,e}
\end{equation}
where $\text{Supply}_e$ is a dummy equal to one for supply-side events and $\mu_e$ are event fixed effects. H4 predicts $\beta_2 > 0$, indicating that the total oil-beta effect ($\beta_1 + \beta_2$) is positive for supply shocks but negative ($\beta_1$ alone) for demand shocks.

\section{Data and methodology}\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Data}

We collect daily returns for 37 US sector and sub-sector ETFs from January 2018 to March 25, 2026 via Yahoo Finance. We apply a liquidity filter requiring average daily dollar volume exceeding \$10 million. After dropping one ETF for insufficient liquidity (HACK) and excluding SPY (used as the market benchmark), the cross-sectional sample comprises 34 ETFs spanning all 11 GICS sectors and 23 sub-industry ETFs.\footnote{The ETF universe includes GICS sector ETFs (XLK, XLF, XLV, XLE, XLI, XLY, XLP, XLRE, XLU, XLB, XLC) and sub-sector ETFs (USO, XOP, FCG, OIH, AMLP, TAN, URA, SMH, IGV, IBB, XBI, IHF, KBE, KRE, KIE, IAI, ITA, XAR, ITB, XHB, IYT, JETS, XRT, REMX). SPY serves as the market return proxy.}

Oil prices (WTI crude), the CBOE Volatility Index (VIX), the US Dollar Index (DXY), and 10-year Treasury yields are obtained from FRED. Economic policy uncertainty \citep{baker2016measuring} provides additional context for the macroeconomic environment surrounding each event. Sector-level mechanism variables---energy intensity, supply chain dependence, and trade exposure---are constructed from the Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA) 2022 Annual Input-Output Tables (Use Table and Total Requirements Table) and Census Bureau trade data \citep{bea2022io}.

\subsection{Event study methodology}

We estimate market-model abnormal returns following \citet{brown1985daily} and \citet{mackinlay1997event}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mktmodel}
    R_{it} = \alpha_i + \beta_i R_{mt} + \varepsilon_{it}
\end{equation}
where the estimation window spans 240 trading days ending 10 days before the event. For the primary Hormuz event, this corresponds to March 3, 2025 to February 12, 2026. Cumulative abnormal returns are computed over the primary $[-10, +10]$ window:
\begin{equation}
    \text{CAR}_i = \sum_{t=-10}^{+10} \hat{\varepsilon}_{it}
\end{equation}
Statistical significance is assessed using \citet{kolari2010event} adjusted $t$-statistics, which account for cross-sectional correlation and event-induced volatility.

\subsection{Multi-factor beta estimation}

To isolate oil price sensitivity from correlated factor exposures, we estimate a five-factor model in the pre-event estimation window:
\begin{equation}
    R_{it} = \alpha_i + \beta_{\text{mkt},i} R_{mt} + \beta_{\text{oil},i} R_{\text{oil},t} + \beta_{\text{vix},i} \Delta\text{VIX}_t + \beta_{\text{dxy},i} R_{\text{dxy},t} + \beta_{\text{y10},i} \Delta Y_{10,t} + \varepsilon_{it}
\end{equation}
where $R_{\text{oil},t}$ is the daily WTI crude oil return. The resulting $\beta_{\text{oil},i}$ measures each ETF's sensitivity to oil price movements, conditional on market, volatility, currency, and interest rate factors.

\subsection{Cross-sectional regression specifications}

We estimate six nested specifications of increasing saturation:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cross}
    \text{CAR}_i = \alpha + \gamma_1 \beta_{\text{oil},i} + \boldsymbol{\gamma}' \mathbf{X}_i + u_i
\end{equation}

Specification~(1) includes only $\beta_{\text{oil}}$. Specification~(2) adds $\beta_{\text{mkt}}$. Specifications~(3)--(4) add macroeconomic factor betas ($\beta_{\text{vix}}$, $\beta_{\text{dxy}}$, $\beta_{\text{y10}}$). Specifications~(5)--(6) add fundamental characteristics: historical volatility, log total assets, debt-to-assets ratio, and price-to-book ratio. All standard errors are HC3-robust \citep{long2000using}, with GICS-sector clustering \citep{cameron2011robust} as a robustness check.

\subsection{Mechanism variables}

To test H2, we construct three sector-level mechanism variables from BEA Input-Output data:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Energy intensity}: The share of petroleum, coal, and utility inputs in total intermediate consumption, computed from the BEA 2022 Use Table. Values range from 2.1\% (Communication Services) to 82.3\% (Energy).
    \item \textbf{Supply chain dependence}: The direct and indirect petroleum requirements coefficient from the BEA 2022 Total Requirements Table, capturing the network propagation of oil shocks through input-output linkages \citep{acemoglu2012network}. Values range from 0.03 (Communication Services) to 0.92 (Energy).
    \item \textbf{Trade exposure}: The ratio of exports plus imports to total shipments, computed from Census Bureau USA Trade Online data. Values range from 2.9\% (Utilities) to 52.8\% (Technology).
\end{itemize}

Sub-sector ETFs inherit the mechanism variable values of their parent GICS sector. All mechanism variables are standardized (mean zero, unit variance) before entering interaction terms.

\subsection{Aggregation Masking Index ($\Psi$)}

We define the bounded Aggregation Masking Index as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:psi}
    \Psi_e = 1 - \frac{\left|\sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i \cdot \text{CAR}_{i,e}\right|}{\sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i \cdot |\text{CAR}_{i,e}|}
\end{equation}
where $w_i$ are capitalization weights that sum to one. By Jensen's inequality, $|\sum w_i \cdot \text{CAR}_i| \leq \sum w_i \cdot |\text{CAR}_i|$, ensuring $\Psi \in [0,1]$. A value of $\Psi = 0$ indicates no masking (all sectors move in the same direction), while $\Psi = 1$ indicates complete masking (the weighted sum of CARs is exactly zero despite nonzero individual CARs). We interpret $\Psi$ as the fraction of sectoral return variation that is concealed through capitalization-weighted aggregation.

We also report the traditional ratio-based index $\Phi = |\text{CAR}_{\text{SPY}}| / \overline{|\text{CAR}_i|}$ for comparison, where values below one indicate that the aggregate index understates average sectoral disruption.

Several features of the data merit discussion. First, the cross-sectional dispersion in CARs is large (standard deviation: 10.47~pp), confirming the heterogeneity central to our analysis. Second, oil betas vary considerably across sectors, from $-0.217$ (IHF, Health Care Providers) to $+0.668$ (XOP, Oil \& Gas Exploration), providing sufficient cross-sectional variation for identification. \subsection{Identification strategy and threats}

Our identification strategy exploits the cross-sectional variation in pre-event oil price sensitivity to explain heterogeneous sectoral responses to an exogenous geopolitical shock. The event-study design mitigates standard endogeneity concerns: the timing and severity of the Hormuz crisis were not caused by sectoral equity returns. However, two identification threats warrant discussion.

First, oil betas may proxy for omitted sector characteristics (e.g., geographic concentration in conflict-affected regions, government contract dependence). We address this by progressively saturating the cross-sectional regression with market beta, macroeconomic factor betas, and fundamental controls (six specifications), showing that $\gamma_1$ attenuates by only 10\% from the baseline to the most demanding specification.

Second, sectors that are expected to benefit from geopolitical tensions may attract speculative flows before the event, potentially contaminating both the estimation-window beta and the event-window CAR. We mitigate this by ending the estimation window 10 trading days before the event and by testing alternative event date definitions, all of which produce consistent results.

\subsection{Event study: Sectoral heterogeneity (H1)}

\subsection{Cross-sectional determinants of CARs}

Table~\ref{tab:crosssection} presents our main cross-sectional results. Across all six specifications, oil beta ($\gamma_1$) is the dominant predictor of CARs, providing strong support for H1.

In the baseline specification including only $\beta_{\text{oil}}$ (Spec~1), $\gamma_1 = +21.45$ ($t = 3.72$, $R^2 = 0.404$). Adding market beta (Spec~2) leaves the estimate virtually unchanged at $+20.18$ ($t = 3.57$, $R^2 = 0.422$). The coefficient remains stable when progressively adding macroeconomic factor betas (Spec~3: $\gamma_1 = +18.38$, $t = 3.52$) and the full suite of fundamental controls (Spec~6: $\gamma_1 = +19.25$, $t = 2.88$, $R^2 = 0.682$). The attenuation from Spec~1 to Spec~6 is only 10.2\%, indicating that oil beta is not proxying for market risk, volatility exposure, firm size, leverage, or valuation.

To address within-sector correlation, we re-estimate Spec~(2) and Spec~(3) with standard errors clustered at the GICS sector level (11 clusters). The clustered $t$-statistics are \textit{larger} than the HC3 values ($t = 6.01$ vs. $3.57$ for Spec~2; $t = 5.01$ vs. $3.52$ for Spec~3), indicating that within-cluster residual correlation is actually negative---sectors within the same GICS group have offsetting residuals, so HC3 standard errors are conservative.

A one-standard-deviation increase in $\beta_{\text{oil}}$ predicts 18--21 percentage points higher CARs over the event window. In tercile splits, high-oil-beta ETFs earned $+4.39\%$ on average versus $-4.85\%$ for low-oil-beta ETFs, a spread of 9.25~pp ($t = 2.98$, $p = 0.008$).

Panel~A plots $\beta_{\text{oil}}$ against CARs, showing a clear positive relationship: sectors with higher pre-event oil price sensitivity experienced more positive (or less negative) abnormal returns. The relationship is approximately linear, is not driven by a small number of outliers, and the regression line passes through the cluster of points rather than being pulled by extremes. Panel~B confirms that market beta ($\beta_{\text{mkt}}$) has no comparable cross-sectional explanatory power.

\subsection{Mechanism identification (H2)}

Table~\ref{tab:mechanism} reports the results of interaction tests designed to identify the economic channels through which oil exposure affects sectoral returns. We test three mechanism variables derived from BEA Input-Output data: energy intensity, supply chain dependence, and trade exposure.

In individual interaction models, the coefficients on the interaction terms are directionally consistent with H2: energy intensity ($+7.11$, $p = 0.268$) and supply chain dependence ($+8.67$, $p = 0.135$) both carry positive signs, suggesting amplification of the oil channel, while trade exposure ($-1.93$, $p = 0.897$) is essentially zero. None are individually significant at conventional levels. We emphasize that this non-significance likely reflects two sources of attenuation: first, insufficient statistical power with $N = 34$ and high correlation between energy intensity and supply chain dependence ($r = 0.97$, as reported in Table~\ref{tab:descriptive}); second, classical measurement error from sub-sector ETFs inheriting parent GICS sector mechanism values (e.g., JETS receives the broad Industrials energy intensity of 11.7\%, whereas airlines' actual fuel cost share is substantially higher). This measurement noise biases interaction coefficients toward zero, making our non-significant results conservative.

The full model including all three mechanism interactions achieves $R^2 = 0.728$, a substantial increase from the baseline $R^2 = 0.422$, indicating that mechanism variables collectively explain considerable cross-sectional heterogeneity. However, severe multicollinearity in the full model (variance inflation factors exceeding 10 for energy intensity and supply chain) renders individual coefficient estimates unstable---the sign reversals between individual and full-model specifications are symptomatic of this collinearity. Accordingly, we interpret the full-model $R^2$ as evidence that mechanism variables \textit{collectively} matter, while relying on the individual interaction models (Specs~1--3) for directional inference about specific channels.

Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism} provides visual evidence of the mechanism channels at the GICS sector level ($N = 11$). Energy intensity and supply chain dependence both exhibit clear positive associations with CARs ($r = 0.65$ and $r = 0.47$, respectively), while trade exposure shows essentially no bivariate relationship ($r = 0.004$). The first two patterns are consistent with H2: sectors with greater real economic exposure to oil---through direct energy costs and indirect input-output linkages---experienced stronger responses to the Hormuz shock. The null result for trade exposure suggests that international trade channels are less relevant than direct energy cost and supply chain channels for transmitting oil shocks to US sectoral equity returns.

\section{External validity: Multi-event evidence}\label{sec:external}

We adopt this approach because our interest is in whether \textit{current} sectoral oil exposure is associated with \textit{past} event responses---i.e., whether the cross-sectional pattern is stable over time. The fact that pre-Hormuz betas predict CARs for events 4--6 years earlier supports the view that relative oil sensitivity is a persistent sector characteristic. Nevertheless, we interpret the historical event results as documenting cross-sectional \textit{associations} rather than out-of-sample \textit{predictions}, and we encourage future work to re-estimate with event-specific betas as a robustness check.}

The panel results provide strong support for H4. In the pooled specification (Spec~1), oil beta is positively and significantly associated with CARs ($\gamma_1 = +7.14$, $t = 1.99$), confirming that the oil channel operates across events. However, the pooled estimate masks substantial heterogeneity across shock types.

Introducing the $\beta_{\text{oil}} \times \text{Supply}$ interaction (Spec~2) reveals a striking asymmetry. The interaction coefficient is $+58.79$ ($t = 5.22$, $p < 0.0001$), our single strongest test statistic. The base coefficient on $\beta_{\text{oil}}$ (the demand-shock effect) is $-39.89$ ($t = -3.22$), while the total supply-shock effect is $-39.89 + 58.79 = +18.90$. This implies:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Supply shocks} ($\gamma_1 = +19.50$ in subsample): High-oil-beta sectors \textit{gain}, while low-oil-beta sectors lose. The shock creates sectoral winners and losers, with oil exposure determining which side of the redistribution each sector falls on.
    \item \textbf{Demand shocks} ($\gamma_1 = -32.61$ in subsample): \textit{All} sectors lose, but high-oil-beta sectors lose disproportionately more. The shock is systemic, and oil exposure amplifies losses rather than creating offsetting effects.
\end{itemize}

This sign flip is consistent with the theoretical predictions of \citet{kilian2009not} and the structural identification of \citet{baumeister2019structural}, who show that supply disruptions redistribute wealth from oil consumers to oil producers, while demand collapses reduce economic activity broadly, with energy-intensive sectors suffering the largest contractions. Cross-sectional evidence from \citet{zaremba2022geopolitical} similarly finds that geopolitical risk changes predict heterogeneous equity returns across markets.

We note an important caveat: the demand-side coefficient is identified from a single event (COVID-19), which was not purely an oil demand shock but a simultaneous health crisis, lockdown, and financial market panic. Accordingly, the negative $\gamma_1$ for the demand subsample may partly reflect COVID-specific factors rather than a general property of demand-side oil shocks. While the supply-side result is robust across four independent events, the demand-side asymmetry should be interpreted as suggestive evidence that warrants confirmation with additional demand-side events (e.g., the 2014--2016 oil glut or the 2008 financial crisis).

Adding market beta as a control (Spec~3) leaves the interaction coefficient unchanged at $+58.79$ ($t = 5.20$), confirming that the shock-type asymmetry is not driven by differential market risk exposure.

The traditional $\Phi$ index yields inconsistent results: only two of five events show $\Phi < 1$ (Hormuz: 0.723; Hamas: 0.996), while the remaining three show $\Phi > 1$. This occurs because $\Phi$ uses the SPY return as the numerator, which can exceed the average absolute sectoral CAR when a systemic component dominates (as in COVID, where all sectors fell sharply).

The bounded $\Psi$ index resolves this limitation. Using the theoretically correct weighted sectoral sum rather than the SPY return, $\Psi$ reveals that \textit{all five events} exhibit strong masking: $\Psi$ ranges from 0.936 (Russia--Ukraine) to 0.960 (Hamas), with a mean of 0.947. This implies that approximately 94.7\% of the underlying sectoral return variation is concealed when individual CARs are aggregated through capitalization weights.

\subsection{Interpretation and the diversification benchmark}

A natural concern is that $\Psi$ may be mechanically high for any diversified portfolio containing assets with mixed-sign returns. This is partially correct: $\Psi$ measures the degree of cancellation in a weighted sum, and diversification is designed to produce exactly such cancellation. However, the relevant question is not whether $\Psi$ is high \textit{per se}, but whether geopolitical shocks generate \textit{systematically higher} cancellation than non-event periods, and whether the sectoral dispersion being cancelled is economically meaningful.

On the first point, we note that the near-invariance of $\Psi$ across events---ranging from 0.936 to 0.960 despite SPY CARs spanning $-1.81\%$ to $-39.68\%$ (a 22-fold difference)---is itself informative. It indicates that geopolitical oil shocks consistently produce the kind of mixed-sign sectoral responses that aggregation eliminates. The placebo analysis in Section~\ref{sec:placebomasking} partially addresses this by showing that the \textit{magnitude} of sectoral dispersion during actual events exceeds most non-event periods, even if the \textit{degree} of cancellation ($\Psi$) may be similar.

On the second point, the economic significance of the cancelled variation is clear from the event study results: the cross-sectional range of CARs during Hormuz exceeds 59 percentage points, with oil-linked ETFs gaining over 42\% while homebuilders lost over 16\%. The fact that these economically consequential responses nearly cancel in the S\&P~500 ($-1.81\%$) is precisely the phenomenon we seek to quantify. We interpret $\Psi$ not as a measure of index ``failure,'' but as a diagnostic indicating the degree to which index-level movements understate the underlying cross-sectional disruption.

\subsection{Alternative benchmarks: Equal-weighted vs. value-weighted}

We compare masking under equal-weighted (EW) and value-weighted (VW) benchmarks. The EW $\Phi$ is substantially lower than the VW $\Phi$ for all events (mean difference: $-1.33$), indicating that equal-weighting produces \textit{more} masking, not less. This is because equal-weighting gives greater relative influence to smaller, more heterogeneous sub-sector ETFs, which diversify more effectively across opposing directions. Both weighting schemes mask the underlying heterogeneity, but through different mechanisms.

\subsection{Placebo analysis}\label{sec:placebomasking}

To assess whether the observed masking is unusual relative to non-event periods, we compute $\Phi$ for 72 randomly selected placebo dates during 2020--2025 (minimum 15-trading-day gap between placebos). The Hormuz $\Phi = 0.723$ ranks at the 29.2nd percentile of the placebo distribution ($\Phi$ mean $= 1.445$, median $= 1.138$), confirming that the Hormuz crisis generated atypically concentrated sectoral disruptions. The joint test requiring both $\Phi \leq 0.723$ and sectoral dispersion $\geq 2.51$ percentage points is satisfied by only 20.8\% of placebos (15/72), indicating that the combination of strong masking and large sectoral disruption is uncommon in normal times.

\subsection{International extension}

As a supplementary analysis, we examine whether aggregation masking extends to the international level. Using 14 country ETFs and World Bank WDI data on energy import dependence, trade openness, and foreign exchange reserves, we estimate cross-country regressions of CARs on national characteristics. In the univariate specification, energy import dependence is strongly significant ($\beta = -0.015$, $t = -7.71$, $p < 0.001$; $R^2 = 0.511$). Adding trade openness and FX reserves raises the $R^2$ to 0.653, with the energy import coefficient remaining significant ($\beta = -0.017$, $t = -4.50$, $p < 0.001$). Countries more reliant on energy imports suffered larger equity losses. The cross-country analogue of $\Phi$ is 0.334, implying even greater masking at the international level---consistent with the broader diversification in global indices concealing substantial cross-country heterogeneity in shock exposure.

% 7. CONCLUSION
% ====================================================================
\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper demonstrates that aggregate market indices systematically mask the heterogeneous effects of geopolitical oil shocks on sectoral equity returns. Using a multi-event framework spanning five major oil-related shocks, we establish four empirical findings with implications for how we measure and interpret the economic impact of geopolitical risk.

First, pre-event oil price sensitivity ($\beta_{\text{oil}}$) is the dominant cross-sectional predictor of sectoral CARs, with a coefficient of $\gamma_1 = +20.18$ ($p < 0.0001$ with GICS-clustered standard errors) that survives six increasingly saturated specifications including market risk, macroeconomic exposures, and firm fundamentals. The result is confirmed by double bootstrap inference (95\% CI: $[+15.25, +31.56]$), leave-one-out analysis, placebo falsification, and alternative event date specifications.

Second, the oil transmission channel operates through identifiable economic mechanisms. While individual interaction terms for energy intensity, supply chain dependence, and trade exposure are not individually significant at $N = 34$, they are directionally consistent with theory and collectively raise the cross-sectional $R^2$ from 0.422 to 0.728. Augmented regressions confirm that $\beta_{\text{oil}}$ survives the inclusion of all mechanism controls ($+4.5\%$ attenuation, $p = 0.002$), indicating that oil exposure is a distinct channel rather than a proxy for sector fundamentals.

Third, our bounded Aggregation Masking Index ($\Psi$) shows that approximately 95\% of the weighted sectoral return dispersion is netted out through capitalization-weighted aggregation, and this holds across all five events regardless of shock type or magnitude ($\Psi$ range: 0.936--0.960). While high $\Psi$ values partly reflect the normal diversification properties of cap-weighted indices, the combination of high $\Psi$ with large, economically meaningful sectoral dispersion (cross-sectional range exceeding 59~pp during Hormuz) indicates that index-level responses substantially understate the underlying cross-sectional disruption. The $\Psi$ metric provides a useful diagnostic for assessing when aggregate indices are informative about true economic impact and when they are not.

Fourth, the multi-event panel (170 observations with event fixed effects) provides cross-sectional evidence consistent with the supply-demand distinction theorized by \citet{kilian2009not}: the $\beta_{\text{oil}} \times \text{Supply}$ interaction is $+58.79$ ($p < 0.0001$), with positive $\gamma_1$ for supply shocks and negative $\gamma_1$ for the COVID demand shock. We note that the demand-side result rests on a single event that was not purely an oil shock, and the asymmetry finding should therefore be treated as suggestive rather than definitive.

These findings carry practical implications. Policymakers who monitor aggregate indices to assess the economic impact of geopolitical crises may substantially underestimate sectoral disruption. Investors who rely on broad market benchmarks for risk assessment may fail to detect the reallocation occurring beneath the index surface. Researchers using aggregate market returns as dependent variables in studies of geopolitical risk may understate true economic effects and lose statistical power.

Several limitations warrant discussion. The cross-sectional sample ($N = 34$ ETFs) limits the power of individual mechanism interaction tests, and sub-sector ETFs inherit parent GICS sector characteristics, introducing measurement noise in the mechanism analysis. The multi-event panel uses pre-Hormuz betas for all events, treating oil sensitivity as time-invariant; while the consistency of results across events supports this assumption, event-specific beta estimation would strengthen the historical event analysis. The demand-side asymmetry (H4) is identified from a single event (COVID-19), limiting the generalizability of the supply-demand distinction. Finally, the $\Psi$ index captures the degree of cancellation in a weighted sum, which is partly mechanical for diversified portfolios; future work should benchmark $\Psi$ against a null distribution from non-event periods to isolate event-specific masking from normal portfolio diversification. As more geopolitical events accumulate, testing $\Psi$'s predictive power for future risk and extending the demand-side sample become natural directions for future research.
